# What Are Circadian Rhythm Disorders? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

What Are Circadian Rhythm Disorders? Symptoms, Causes, Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention

Falling asleep too early in the evening, struggling to wake up in the morning, or dealing with lethargy throughout all hours of the day can all be symptomatic of circadian rhythm disorders.

Circadian rhythm disorders — also known as sleep-wake cycle disorders — are a group of conditions sharing one common feature: a disruption to the body’s internal clock and, in turn, timing of sleep as your sleep-wake cues fall out of sync with your environment.

“Your circadian rhythm is your internal clock that goes through a 24-hour cycle, aligning with what’s happening during your day, from alertness to hunger to the release of hormones to prepare for sleep. To feel your best, you need this internal clock to be aligned with your external clock,” says John Cline, PhD, a New Haven, Connecticut–based clinical psychologist who specializes in treating sleep disorders.

When your internal clock is out of sync with your external schedule, it can lead to numerous health problems, adds Marie-Pierre St-Onge, PhD, director of the Sleep Center of Excellence at Columbia University Irving Medical Center in New York City.

With these disorders, someone is feeling the urge to sleep at times that are incompatible with their lifestyle, Dr. St-Onge says. For example, you could be falling asleep by 7 p.m. and waking up at 3 a.m. when you really need to be awake later in the evenings and asleep for more of the morning, she explains.

Signs and Symptoms of Circadian Rhythm Disorders

The symptoms you’ll encounter will depend on the type of circadian rhythm disorder you have and how severe the condition is, Dr. Cline says.

According to Cleveland Clinic and National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI), general symptoms of circadian rhythm sleep disorders can include:

Insomnia (when you have difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep)

What Are the Different Types of Circadian Rhythm Disorders?

Across the board, circadian rhythm disorders disrupt a traditional sleep-wake routine, but in different ways. The six most common are:

Advanced Sleep-Wake Phase Disorder (ASWPD) People grappling with ASWPD fall asleep early in the evening around 6 p.m. to 9 p.m., and wake up in the early morning around 2 a.m. to 5 a.m., according to Stanford Medicine.

Delayed Sleep-Wake Phase Disorder (DSWPD) DSWPD is one of the most common circadian rhythm disorders, often afflicting adolescents, teenagers, and young adults, according to Cline. In this case, you’ll go to sleep and wake up more than two hours later than what is typically considered a “normal” sleep-wake cycle. Lots of people end up staying up later than usual from time to time because of work or school responsibilities or socializing. This type of delayed sleep pattern may count as a clinical sleep disorder if it affects your performance at work or school, or the health of your relationships, according to Eric Landsness, MD, PhD, an assistant professor of neurology and sleep medicine at the Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis. “Night owls can leverage their tendency to sleep in the day and then work the night shift. It’s when you try to force yourself onto a schedule that isn’t aligned with what your internal clock wants that you get into trouble,” Dr. Landsness says.

Irregular Sleep-Wake Rhythm Disorder (ISWRD) People with ISWRD oscillate between short periods of sleep and wakefulness with an undefined sleep-wake cycle. “There is simply no consistent sleep period. [It] happens at any time during 24 hours,” says Hussam Al-Sharif, MD, a pulmonologist and sleep medicine specialist at the Mayo Clinic in Eau Claire, Wisconsin. It typically occurs in people with a neurological disorder, like dementia or Alzheimer’s disease, who also don’t have a regular daytime routine, research suggests.

Non-24-Hour Sleep-Wake Rhythm Disorder People living with non-24-hour sleep-wake syndrome tend to keep the same length of sleep and awake time, but their internal clocks run for longer than 24 hours. When this happens, their sleep times steadily become more delayed, according to Dr. Al-Sharif.

Jet Lag Disorder If you’ve flown at least two time zones away, you will likely have encountered temporary jet lag disorder. In this case, your sleep-wake rhythm falls out of sync with the local time at your new destination as you change time zones, throwing off when you feel awake, sleepy, and even hungry, according to Cline.

Shift Work Disorder People who work overnight shifts or are on a rotating, unfixed work schedule are most susceptible to shift work disorder. There are several variations of shift work schedules that can cause difficulties, including early morning shifts, night shifts, overnight shifts and rotating shifts.

Causes and Risk Factors of Circadian Rhythm Disorders?

Many factors could be at play behind the onset of circadian rhythm disorders, according to Landsness.

“Most of the time you are genetically predisposed and some event — illness, emotional stress, or a change in job — triggers you to fall into a maladaptive behavior,” Landsness says. But other factors, like comorbidities, can contribute to circadian rhythm disorders, too.

Here's a look at the key causes behind circadian rhythm disorders, according to the NHLBI:

Genetics Your genes may predispose you to sleep-wake disorders if, for example, you naturally like to wake up early or stay up late. Some people can adjust their body’s clock and schedule to match their environment a lot easier than others, too.

Age The rhythm and timing of your sleep-wake cycle will change throughout your life due to changes in your brain. Teens, for example, tend to want to naturally sleep later compared to adults, which makes them more vulnerable to delayed sleep-wake phase disorder.

Occupation People who work overnight shifts, and even early morning and late night shifts are the prime candidates for shift work disorder. Meanwhile, pilots, flight attendants, athletes, and people who travel around the world frequently for business are at an increased risk of jet lag disorder.

Lifestyle What you do during your waking hours can predispose you to circadian rhythm disorders, too. Factors like alcohol use, chronic caffeine consumption, substance abuse, frequent air travel, and lack of exposure to natural light during the day are key examples. You could also have poor sleep hygiene, such as scrolling through your phone right before bed, or being exposed to artificial light at night time. A busy social schedule that keeps you out partying on the weekends can also spell trouble for your sleep during the workweek, Cline says.

Comorbidities Cognitive impairment, stemming from dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, or brain injury, may put people at a higher risk of sleep-wake disorders, according to research published in the journal Experimental & Molecular Medicine. This may be because of changes to the brain, particularly tied to melatonin secretion, and feelings of confusion and disorientation at night. People grappling with mental health issues, such as depression or anxiety, are also at a higher risk of circadian rhythm disorders, especially delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, according to a study published in The Lancet Psychiatry.

How Are Circadian Rhythm Disorders Diagnosed?

If you think you may be dealing with a sleep-wake disorder, symptoms must be ongoing for at least one to three months, according to Cline. If, however, the symptoms are severely interrupting your life, or putting your health at risk, seek medical attention sooner, he suggests.

Circadian rhythm disorders are diagnosed by a combination of looking through a patient’s medical history, weekly routine, travel history, and via records obtained from wearing an actigraphy, which is worn on your wrist to collect information, such as light exposure, activity, wake-up times and bedtimes, according to Al-Sharif.

Your family doctor may also refer to a sleep specialist who will ask you to log your daily sleep routine in a sleep diary for a few weeks, Landsness says.

During an appointment with your doctor, he or she may zero in on:

When, for how long, and how well you sleep

Your symptoms when they began

Your personal and family medical history

Your use of caffeine, nicotine, alcohol, or illegal drugs

Prognosis of Circadian Rhythm Disorders

There are many different types of circadian rhythm disorders, each with their own features, treatment options, and effects on day-to-day living. Some can be resolved and go away completely with appropriate treatment (like jet lag), while others can be chronic and require ongoing management.

Duration of Circadian Rhythm Disorders

Cline says circadian rhythm disorders can afflict people for weeks to months, depending on whether treatment is effective. Others may be brought on by another comorbidity or be a result of aging, and be chronic.

It’s important to follow your doctor’s prescribed treatment plan if you’ve been diagnosed with a circadian rhythm disorder, and let your provider know if your symptoms change or worsen, per the NHLBI.

Treatment and Medication Options for Circadian Rhythm Disorders

Circadian rhythm disorders are treated in a variety of ways. Sometimes behavioral and lifestyle changes alone can help; in other cases medications or other treatments are needed.

Lifestyle and Behavioral Changes That Can Help

Light therapy involves exposure to artificial light that resembles sunlight. Typically, patients keep a light therapy lamp close to them so the light is in their peripheral vision, according to the Sleep Foundation. About 20 minutes of light therapy in the evenings can push your bedtime later in the evening, according to Cline. Additionally, light exposure first thing in the morning can help you push your wake up time earlier, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Keeping a consistent sleep-wake schedule (or going to bed and waking up at roughly the same time each day) can help, Al-Sharif says. Maintaining the timing of the body’s internal clock helps the body establish a routine, helping you to fall asleep and wake up more easily, according to Harvard Medical School. That means you’ll have to give up sleeping in on the weekends, Cline notes.

Improving your sleep hygiene is something your sleep specialist may also work with you on, according to Al-Sharif. He or she may, for example, encourage you to avoid caffeine in the second half of the day, drive home with sunglasses after an overnight shift to limit exposure to sunlight, and use blackout curtains in the bedroom to block out all natural light if you’re trying to sleep before an overnight shift.

Medication Options

Before taking any medications consult with your sleep specialist to find safe options, and make sure you understand the possible side effects, Cline says.

Melatonin Cline says sleep specialists may use melatonin to treat sleep-wake disorders. Melatonin supplements are lab-made versions of this sleep hormone that your doctor may prescribe for you to use to help you feel drowsy at the correct bedtime.

Melatonin Receptor Agonists Cline also says doctors may also turn to melatonin receptor agonists, which past research suggests has been successful in safely and effectively treating insomnia, delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, and non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder. They include: ramelteon, prolonged-release melatonin, agomelatine, and tasimelteon.

Sleep-Promoting Medicine Sleeping pills can be prescribed temporarily, but are often used as a last resort because of the risk of drug dependency, Cline says. Benzodiazepines and zolpidem are two of the most common medications that can help people with insomnia or circadian rhythm disorders fall asleep faster and stay asleep longer, according to the NHLBI. Sleeping pill side effects can include muscle weakness and confusion, which can be more severe in seniors and people diagnosed with dementia, per NHLBI.

Wake-Promoting Medicine On the other end of the spectrum is medication that helps patients stay awake and alert if they’re dealing with shift work disorder or excessive daytime sleepiness, according to research. Modafinil, armodafinil, and dextroamphetamine are the most commonly used drugs in this category.

Alternative and Complementary Therapies

Chronotherapy is a behavioral approach to resetting your circadian clock by delaying sleep and wake times by roughly three hours every two days until you hit your desired sleep and wake times, according to past research. This is a demanding way to adjust your sleep pattern, though — you and your family will need to commit to adjusting to this new schedule, avoid bright light in the evenings, and stick to strict sleep hygiene habits.

Yoga and exercise can help, too, usually alongside other therapies. Research shows that yoga can improve sleep quality, from lowering stress to getting into the habit of practicing mindfulness. The Sleep Foundation recommends hatha yoga and nidra yoga, which include gentle body postures and calming breathing techniques that can help to relax your body and prepare you for bedtime. Your doctor or sleep specialist may even recommend a regular exercise routine — the Sleep Foundation suggests working out can improve sleep quality and cut excessive daytime sleepiness for people with insomnia.

Prevention of Circadian Rhythm Disorders

If you’re worried about encountering a sleep-wake disorder, or you’re at an increased risk of sleep-wake disorders, your doctor may suggest important lifestyle changes to keep your body clock running on time. They include:

Maintaining a Regular Bedtime and Mealtime Schedule This advice comes up multiple times in this article because it’s one of the top ways to support a healthy sleep schedule. Keeping a consistent routine when it comes to going to bed and waking up is crucial to maintaining a healthy circadian rhythm. This point especially applies to people working the night shift or who are on a rotating schedule, Cline says. “Routinizing and regularizing your schedule, including timekeepers like eating breakfast at the exact same time everyday will help,” Cline says.

Avoiding Napping or Hitting the Snooze Button Taking naps during the day or sleeping in on the weekends will also interrupt your body’s internal clock. Try to limit these bad habits if you’re concerned about delayed sleep-wake phase disorder.

Limiting Your Caffeine Intake, Especially Before Bedtime The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommends around 400 milligrams (mg) — or four to five cups of coffee per day, maximum. The FDA notes a large cup of coffee can have up to 470 mg of caffeine, which is already more than the daily recommended dosage. You should cut off caffeine about six hours before bedtime, if not sooner, according to the Sleep Foundation.

Keeping an Eye on Alcohol Consumption Alcohol has sedative effects that can help you relax and wind down, but too much drinking has been linked to interrupted sleep, poor sleep quality, and less sleep, according to the Sleep Foundation.

Adjusting Your Sleep Schedule Before Long Distance Travel If you’re worried about jet lag disorder, gradually begin adjusting your sleep-wake cycle days to match the time at your destination. You can use bright light therapy and slowly shift your wake and sleep times by 30 minutes at a time to help advance or delay your internal clock, Cline says.

Staying Away From Multiple Schedule Switches Some people cannot adjust to overnight shifts, or a constantly changing work schedule, Cline says. “Some people adjust well to [irregular work schedules] and for others it’s much harder.”

Complications of Circadian Rhythm Disorders

Because circadian rhythms often cause sleep deprivation, they can bring on many of the same problems that are linked to not getting enough sleep, among others. Some complications linked to circadian rhythm disorders are:

Increased Risk of Work- or Driving-Related Accidents A library of research has connected shift work sleep disorder with car crashes. A study published in 2021 in the journal Safety Science, for example, found that drivers who suffer from shift work sleep disorder are nearly 3 times more likely to be involved in a car accident compared with drivers without a sleep disorder. Whether you’re battling shift work disorder or any other sleep-wake disorder, sleep deprived employees are 70 percent more likely to be involved in workplace accidents or encounter workplace injury compared to well-rested employees, research suggests.

Fertility Problems Research suggests shift work can prolong the waiting time to get pregnant because of menstrual irregularities and hormonal imbalances tied to circadian rhythm disturbances. Shift work, jet lag, and even daylight saving time can throw off women’s circadian rhythm so much that they’ve been associated with decreased fertility and increased rates of miscarriage, according to a study published in 2019 in the journal Current Opinion in Endocrinology, Diabetes, and Obesity.

Digestive Disorders With sleep and appetite regulation out of sync, digestive issues may crop up too, including stomach ulcers, constipation, diarrhea and irritable bowel syndrome, research has found.

Cognitive and Behavioral Issues Ongoing sleep deprivation may contribute to decreases in attention span, concentration, motor skills and memory, according to the NHLBI. These complications can reduce productivity and increase workplace mistakes.

Mood Disorders Battling circadian rhythm irregularities and a lack of sleep infringing on your work and personal life may make it more challenging to manage mental health. Complications may include mood disorders, such as depression, anxiety and bipolar disorder, and seasonal affective disorder, according to Harvard Health Publishing.

Cancer and Cardiovascular Disease A growing body of research suggests shift work can increase cancer risk, especially in women working the overnight shift. A study that looked at nearly four million women, published in 2019 in the journal Cancer Epidemiology, Biomarkers, and Prevention, found that long-term night shift work among women increased cancer risk by 19 percent. The study found that women who work the night shift had a 41 percent increased risk of skin cancer, a 32 percent increased risk of breast cancer, and an 18 percent increased risk of gastrointestinal cancer. For every five years of night shift work, the risk for breast cancer increased by 3.3 percent. The CDC says that in addition to cancer, night shift work has been associated with type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and stroke.

Research and Statistics: How Many People Have Circadian Rhythm Disorders?

Research suggests that up to 3 percent of the adult population suffers from a circadian rhythm sleep disorder, but the rate may be higher because these sleep-wake disorders are often confused with insomnia and underdiagnosed.

The prevalence also increases in people who are visually impaired. According to research published in the journal BMC Public Health, sleep-wake disorders in the blind was 33.1 percent, with non-24-hour sleep-wake disorder being the most common at 26.8 percent, followed by advanced sleep-wake phase disorder and delayed sleep-wake phase disorder.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) (PDF) says that delayed sleep-wake phase disorder is most common among adolescents, teenagers and young adults, with a prevalence of seven to 16 percent. On the other hand, advanced sleep-wake phase disorder tends to afflict middle-aged and older adults; the AASM estimates that approximately one percent of this group are affected.

People with diagnosed mental illnesses, such as mood or anxiety disorders, are also more likely to have a circadian rhythm sleep disorder — research suggests 4.5 percent of people in this group also have sleep-wake disorders.

Related Conditions and Causes of Circadian Rhythm Disorders

According to the NHLBI, several medical conditions can increase your risk of circadian rhythm disorders, including:

Autism spectrum disorder

Conditions that affect eyesight and access to natural light, including blindness and macular degeneration

Conditions that affect mental health, including depression, anxiety and schizophrenia

Conditions that affect cognitive health, including dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease

Certain genetic conditions, such as Huntington’s disease, Smith-Magenis syndrome, and Angelman syndrome
