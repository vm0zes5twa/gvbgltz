NAD+ vs. Glutathione IV Therapy: Which One Is Right for You?

Choosing between NAD+ IV therapy and glutathione IV therapy isn’t straightforward; that’s because both are popular for good reasons. If you’ve been researching energy, longevity, detox, or recovery treatments, you’ve likely seen both recommended, sometimes interchangeably. That creates a real decision problem: they’re often grouped together, but they work very differently.

This guide is designed to give you a clear, practical answer. Not just what each therapy does, but when it makes sense to choose one over the other, and when combining them is the smarter move.

A key piece of context: levels of both NAD+ and glutathione decline significantly with age. Research suggests NAD+ levels can drop by roughly 50% between ages 20 and 60, while glutathione declines in parallel due to oxidative stress, toxin exposure, and reduced production. This is why both therapies are often discussed in longevity and performance circles, not as competitors, but as complementary tools.

What Does NAD+ Do?

NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a coenzyme found in every cell of your body. Its primary role is to facilitate cellular energy production and support DNA repair.

At a basic level, NAD+ helps convert nutrients into ATP, the energy currency your cells use to function. Without sufficient NAD+, your cells literally cannot produce energy efficiently.

NAD+ is also essential for activating proteins called sirtuins, which are involved in aging, inflammation regulation, and cellular repair processes. This is one reason it’s often associated with longevity-focused therapies.

What Does Glutathione Do?

Glutathione is often called the body’s “master antioxidant”, and for good reason. It plays a central role in neutralizing free radicals, detoxifying harmful compounds, and supporting immune function.

Unlike NAD+, which drives energy production, glutathione’s primary role is protection.

Why Glutathione Levels Decline

Glutathione levels decrease due to:

Aging

Chronic stress

Environmental toxins

Poor diet

Low glutathione levels are associated with increased oxidative stress, which contributes to aging and chronic disease processes.

Why IV (or Injection) Delivery Is Preferred

Oral glutathione has extremely low bioavailability—less than 1%—because it is broken down by enzymes in the digestive tract before reaching the bloodstream. [1]

This is why IV and injectable glutathione are considered far more effective delivery methods.

The Case for Combining Both

This is where the conversation becomes more interesting, and more useful.

NAD+ and glutathione are not redundant. They are biologically connected.

The Biochemical Relationship (Simplified)

NAD+ metabolism produces NADPH

NADPH is required to keep glutathione in its active (reduced) form

Active glutathione neutralizes oxidative stress

Reduced oxidative stress helps preserve NAD+ levels

In other words: NAD+ helps keep glutathione working. Glutathione helps protect NAD+.

This creates a reinforcing cycle.
