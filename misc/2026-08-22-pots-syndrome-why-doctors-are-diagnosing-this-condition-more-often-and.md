Explanation

Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome (POTS syndrome) is a form of dysautonomia—an abnormality of the autonomic nervous system. Over the past decade, more people are being diagnosed with POTS syndrome, and for good reasons: greater awareness, better diagnostic tools, and a growing understanding of triggers such as viral infections (including long COVID). Below, we explore why this uptick in diagnosis is happening and outline a clear, practical treatment plan.

Why POTS Syndrome Diagnoses Are Rising

Improved Awareness Among Clinicians

Specialty groups (e.g., Dysautonomia International) have published guidelines that highlight POTS syndrome.

Medical conferences and journals now feature more studies on autonomic dysfunction.

Better Diagnostic Tools and Criteria

Tilt table tests are more widely available.

The 2015 Heart Rhythm Society criteria set clear heart-rate and symptom thresholds for diagnosis.

Recognition of Post-Viral and Post-COVID Cases

A subset of patients develops POTS syndrome after viral infections, including SARS-CoV-2.

Research from NIH and other institutes reports autonomic nervous system changes following certain infections.

Multidisciplinary Dysautonomia Clinics

Hospitals now host dedicated autonomic disorder clinics, speeding up referrals.

Teams often include neurologists, cardiologists, physical therapists, and dietitians.

What Happens in POTS Syndrome?

POTS syndrome stems from poor regulation of blood flow and heart rate upon standing. Key features include:

Excessive Heart-Rate Increase: A jump of 30 beats per minute (or over 120 bpm total) within 10 minutes of standing.

Blood-Volume Pooling: Blood pools in the legs and abdomen, reducing return to the heart.

Autonomic Nervous System Imbalance: Overactive "fight-or-flight" responses and underactive "rest-and-digest" responses.

Common symptoms:

Lightheadedness or near-fainting when standing

Rapid heartbeat (palpitations)

Fatigue or "brain fog"

Headaches and migraines

Gastrointestinal issues (nausea, bloating)

Exercise intolerance

Temperature regulation problems

Detailed Medical History

Document onset of symptoms, triggers, and any prior viral illnesses.

Note family history of autonomic disorders or connective-tissue diseases.

Physical Examination and Orthostatic Vital Signs

Measure blood pressure and heart rate while supine and after standing at 1-, 3-, and 10-minute marks.

Rule out dehydration, anemia, thyroid disorders, or adrenal issues.

Tilt Table Test (if available)

Confirms diagnosis by simulating the change in posture under controlled conditions.

Basic Laboratory Workup

CBC (complete blood count), basic metabolic panel, thyroid and adrenal function tests.

Rule out secondary causes such as pheochromocytoma or diabetes.

Comprehensive Treatment Plan for POTS Syndrome

There's no one-size-fits-all cure, but many patients achieve significant relief through a combination of lifestyle changes, physical conditioning, and medications.

1. Lifestyle and Dietary Modifications

Increase Fluid Intake• Aim for 2–3 liters of water daily.• Consider electrolyte-rich drinks (low-sugar sports beverages).

Boost Salt Intake• Unless contraindicated by high blood pressure or kidney issues, target 8–10 grams of salt per day.• Use salt tablets if needed (under medical supervision).

Frequent Small Meals• Avoid large carbohydrate-heavy meals that can worsen blood pooling.• Incorporate protein and healthy fats.

Compression Garments• Thigh-high or waist-high compression stockings (20–30 mmHg).• Abdomen binders can help reduce pooling.

Sleep and Stress Hygiene• Elevate the head of the bed by 4–6 inches to reduce morning orthostatic stress.• Practice relaxation techniques (deep breathing, meditation).

2. Graded Exercise Program

Building tolerance gradually is crucial:

Phase 1: Recumbent Activities• Start with recumbent biking, rowing, or swimming.• Aim for 10–15 minutes daily, 4–5 times a week.

Phase 2: Semi-Erect Exercises• Progress to seated or inclined recumbent bike, leg exercises while seated.• Increase duration by 1–2 minutes each session.

Phase 3: Upright Conditioning• Slowly introduce upright stationary cycling, walking.• Monitor heart rate to avoid excessive tachycardia.

3. Medications

Prescribed based on individual needs and coexisting conditions:

Fludrocortisone: Helps retain sodium and water, expanding blood volume.

Midodrine: A vasoconstrictor that reduces blood pooling in extremities.

Beta-Blockers (e.g., Propranolol, Metoprolol): Control heart-rate spikes; start at low doses.

Ivabradine: Specifically slows heart rate without affecting blood pressure.

Pyridostigmine: Improves autonomic transmission, may reduce symptoms in some patients.

Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs): For patients with overlapping anxiety symptoms; can help stabilize autonomic tone.

4. Advanced and Adjunctive Therapies

Intravenous (IV) Saline Infusions: Short-term relief for patients who struggle to maintain blood volume orally.

Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation (TENS): May improve circulation in the legs.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): Supports coping skills, especially when anxiety or depression coexists.

Immunotherapy: Under investigation in select autoimmune-driven POTS cases.

Monitoring Progress and Adjusting Care

Keep a symptom diary: Note daily heart rate, fluid and salt intake, exercise, and symptom severity.

Regular follow-ups: Adjust medications, evaluate side effects, and adapt the exercise program.

Work with a multidisciplinary team: Cardiologist, neurologist, physical therapist, dietitian, and mental-health professional.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

While POTS syndrome itself is rarely life-threatening, severe symptoms can mimic or overlap with other serious conditions. Contact a doctor if you experience:

Chest pain or pressure

Severe shortness of breath at rest

Fainting without warning

Sudden, severe headache

New neurological deficits (e.g., weakness, vision changes)
