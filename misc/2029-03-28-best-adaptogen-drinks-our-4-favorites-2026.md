How we evaluated adaptogen drinks

When evaluating adaptogen drinks on the market, we considered five customer-centric criteria: safety, potential effectiveness, cost, convenience, and taste. The basics of each criterion are as follows:

Safety: Does the product go through any testing? Are there allergens present? Do any of the ingredients pose a hazard to consumers? Has the company obtained any safety certifications for the product or for its manufacturing (GMP, ISO, NSF, Informed Sport, etc.)?

Effectiveness: Is the company transparent about its ingredients and/or their doses? Are the product ingredients supported by scientific evidence? Do the doses match up, exceed, or come close to those used in successful studies or trials?

Cost: How affordable (or not) is the product? Does the pricing seem reasonable compared to what the product offers? Are there subscription deals or bundles available?

Convenience: How easily can the product fit into a customer’s routine? Does ordering from the company present any hurdles that may be frustrating to consumers? Do subscription intervals line up properly with the amount of product servings?

Taste: What flavors is the product available in? Is there anything noticeable about the flavor that a customer may want to know before purchasing? Can the flavor of the product be altered?

What are adaptogens?

Basically, adaptogens are botanicals (usually plants or mushrooms) that can help your body “adapt” to or recover from mental or physical stressors.7 For example, some adaptogens may reduce cortisol levels and help you relax after a stressful day,8 while others might lower inflammation when you’re recovering after intense exercise.9

According to the Cleveland Clinic and doctors from the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, an adaptogen must have three qualities:7 10

It’s non-toxic when taken in normal doses

It helps your body cope with stress

It can assist your body in returning to balance (i.e., it should “temper that which is hyperfunctioning and support that which is hypofunctioning”)10

Who should look elsewhere?

There are a few misconceptions surrounding adaptogen drinks that, when clarified, could mean someone would be better off looking elsewhere. One misconception is that, since adaptogen drinks are promoted as an alternative to alcohol, they can provide a similar feeling or “buzz.”

While certain adaptogen drinks — like some of those from BRĒZ — may include ingredients like THC and CBD that can alter your state of mind, they won’t cause the same type of feeling as alcohol. And in drinks that don’t contain cannabis, you may feel more relaxed from certain adaptogens, but you shouldn’t feel anywhere close to “buzzed” or “drunk.”

Insider Tip: BRĒZ only sells its drinks to those who are 21 and older, even if the drink variety you’re looking to purchase is THC- and CBD-free. So, if you’re under 21, you’ll have to shop elsewhere for an adaptogen drink.

Another misconception is that, since they’re non-alcoholic, adaptogen drinks should be safe for children or those who are pregnant or breastfeeding — but this is not the case. There hasn’t been nearly enough research on whether or not most adaptogens and other botanicals are safe for kids or teens, and the doses in adaptogen drinks are intended for adults, meaning they could be far too high for young people.13 14

When it comes to pregnancy or breastfeeding, similar factors apply. For example, some research suggests that ashwagandha could pose a risk to your baby, but the evidence is mixed, and far more research is necessary.15 63 Other botanicals haven’t had their safety for pregnant or breastfeeding people determined by studies or trials.13 Ultimately, it’s best to speak with your doctor before trying adaptogen drinks, whether you’re pregnant or breastfeeding (or neither).

Are adaptogen drinks safe?

For most healthy adults, adaptogen drinks should be safe to drink in moderation. It’s important to remember that consuming an adaptogen drink is basically the same as taking adaptogen supplements, and too high a dose of those botanical ingredients could lead to adverse effects. This is why one of the qualities that makes a plant an adaptogen is specifically that it must be “non-toxic when taken in normal doses.”7 10

Multiple studies have examined the safety of various adaptogens, and the findings have been generally positive. As noted in a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis, “On the basis of studies included in the systematic review, adaptogens can be considered generally safe.” The review authors examined 25 studies on nine different adaptogens (some more common than others), including bacopa, eleuthero, longjack, Jiaogulan or “southern ginseng,” maca, holy basil, ginseng, rhodiola, and ashwagandha.16

The overall findings for each adaptogen are explained in the text and detailed below:16

Ashwagandha: Out of nine trials, nearly all of them reported no adverse effects; only one study reported mild “giddiness, heaviness of head, blurred vision, and/or hyperacidity” in two subjects (4% of participants).

Bacopa: From two studies on bacopa, one noted no adverse effects, while the other only reported mild gastrointestinal disturbances.

Eleuthero: One trial reported no severe adverse effects, but another noted a few cases of breast tenderness and uterine bleeding.

Holy basil: In two trials, no adverse events were recorded, but a later trial had some reports of headache, indigestion, and (in one participant) increased agitation.

Ginseng: Though two trials found no adverse effects, one study reported 19 adverse events in eight of the 19 subjects, all of which were determined to be unrelated to the ginseng.

Rhodiola: Two clinical trials found no adverse effects, but 10% of participants in another trial reported forgetfulness, loss of appetite, or “metabolic and nutritional disturbances.”

Maca: No adverse events were reported in the examined study.

Longjack: The study found no adverse effects.

Jiaogulan: Though no serious events occurred, 14 mild adverse events were reported, but researchers determined that they weren’t related to the treatment.

Similarly, in an earlier review from 2010, the authors explain that the adaptogens eleuthero, rhodiola, and schisandra were “reported to be safe in acute and subacute toxicity studies.” Moreover, some research suggests that rhodiola may even have anti-toxic activity, but more studies are needed.8

However, even when consumed as directed, adaptogen drinks still pose some considerable health risks for certain populations. These include, but aren’t limited to:

Pregnant or nursing individuals

There haven’t been enough studies conducted on the safety of adaptogen use in pregnant or nursing individuals, but some of the research that does exist suggests proceeding with caution.13 15 Besides botanicals, other ingredients in adaptogen drinks could also pose a risk. As an example, several BRĒZ products contain THC, which can harm a developing fetus and pass into breast milk.17

Children and adolescents

Adaptogen drinks aren’t intended for those under 18 (or 21, in some cases) and shouldn’t be consumed by children or teens. Most importantly, there hasn’t been enough research into whether or not adaptogens are safe for young people.13 But another issue is the fact that — even if the ingredients were safe — the doses are intended for adults and may be too high for smaller bodies to handle.

People taking various medications

Adaptogens can interact with a wide range of medications, from antidiabetic drugs and anticonvulsants to sedatives, thyroid hormones, anticoagulants, blood pressure drugs, and more.18 19 20

As detailed in a 2023 retrospective, adaptogens of various types “were involved in 9% of adverse events associated with the concomitant use of antidepressants and other preparations.” Though the adverse events are rare, it’s worth proceeding with caution when using any sort of adaptogen product alongside an antidepressant — or any other medication.21

Those with medical conditions

As with medications, adaptogenic ingredients can also interact with medical conditions. For example, when it comes to functional mushrooms, reishi can lower blood sugar, reduce blood pressure, or slow your blood’s ability to clot;22 chaga should be avoided by those with kidney problems due to its high oxalate levels;23 cordyceps may exacerbate multiple sclerosis (MS), lupus, or other autoimmune conditions;24 and lion’s mane can affect blood sugar levels and increase the risk of organ or tissue transplant rejection.25 26

Of course, the above list of safety concerns isn’t conclusive, so it’s important to speak with your doctor first before adding any new supplements to your routine — including adaptogen drinks, no matter how safe they may appear.

How do adaptogen drinks work?

Each adaptogen drink “works” differently based on the ingredients the company uses in its formula(s). Depending on the manufacturer’s intentions, adaptogen drinks may contain a variety of botanical inclusions — often plants and/or functional mushrooms. In addition to adaptogens, some of the drinks may contain nootropics, vitamins, minerals, amino acids, or other potentially beneficial ingredients. However, in this guide, we’re mainly focusing on the adaptogens.

Below, we break down some of the research behind many of the star adaptogenic ingredients found in our top picks and others on the market.

Ashwagandha

Also called Withania somnifera, ashwagandha is perhaps best known for its potential to relieve anxiety, improve sleep, and regulate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.27 In more recent research, ashwagandha has demonstrated an ability to reduce self-reported anxiety levels and improve subjects’ sleep quality.28 29

Rhodiola

Similar to ashwagandha, rhodiola has performed well in research investigating it as an anxiety reducer.30 In addition to anti-anxiety effects, other studies have shown that rhodiola may improve insomnia, emotional stability, and depression, with one study finding rhodiola was only slightly less effective than sertraline (an SSRI antidepressant medication) in treating major depressive disorder.31 32

Bacopa

More large-scale research is needed to determine the cognitive effects of taking bacopa, but some studies have found that it might be able to improve subjects’ task accuracy, enhance attention, and boost working memory.33 34

Ginseng

Unfortunately, ginseng has perhaps the least promising data out of the mentioned ingredients. While some studies have found improvements in memory recall, concentration, and other aspects of cognition,35 36 a 2010 meta-analysis on ginseng notes that very few trials met its data reliability standards.37

Lion’s mane

All parts of the lion’s mane mushroom may be able to stimulate the production of nerve growth factor (NGF), which could explain why it appears to promote peripheral nerve regeneration.38 39

Trials using lion’s mane have also found that it may reduce the symptoms of anxiety and depression with daily use.4 But other studies on the mushroom for cognition have produced mixed findings, with one noting improvements and another resulting in positive, null, and “limited negative findings.”40 41

Cordyceps

Much of the positive research on cordyceps has focused on athletic performance. For example, one study found that giving 20 healthy older adults cordyceps extract daily for four months improved their physical performance.42 Some experts attribute the endurance- and strength-boosting potential of cordyceps to its anti-stress properties.43 44

More human studies are needed, but research suggests cordyceps may also be able to reduce inflammation, improve immune function, and inhibit tumors, among other things.45

Reishi

In a 2018 review, researchers note that reishi mushrooms are one of many adaptogens that can support adrenal function and reduce arthritis-associated inflammation. The former is particularly interesting, as the body’s adrenal glands control how much cortisol is released in response to stress.46 47

One small 2014 study examined the effects of treating seven healthy male amateur cyclists for three months with a combination of reishi and cordyceps. The results demonstrated “an increased scavenger capacity of free radicals” in the subjects’ serum after cycling, “thereby protecting the athletes from oxidative stress.”48

Even though adaptogens have grown in popularity over the past few years, you may have noticed that a lot of the research on the above ingredients is still in the early stages. Much of the current information is promising, but more large-scale human studies and trials are needed.
