# Aortic Aneurysm: Who Should Be Screened and What the Numbers Mean | Ubie Doctor's Note

Aortic Aneurysm: Who Should Be Screened and What the Numbers Mean

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Who should be screened for an aortic aneurysm? Screening for abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is recommended for men ages 65–75 who have ever smoked. It may also be considered for never-smoking men and women in the same age range with risk factors such as a family history of aneurysm, heart or peripheral artery disease, or high blood pressure. Thoracic aortic aneurysm screening is recommended for people with genetic conditions like Marfan syndrome, a family history of thoracic aneurysm or dissection, or a bicuspid aortic valve.

How are aortic aneurysms monitored and treated? Aneurysm size and growth rate determine monitoring intervals and surgical referral thresholds. Below, you'll find a complete breakdown of measurement categories, follow-up schedules, lifestyle modifications, medication options, and treatment approaches.

Aortic aneurysms are often called "silent killers" because they can grow undetected until rupture, which is frequently fatal. If you're experiencing warning signs like abdominal or back pain, a pulsating sensation near the navel, or unexplained chest discomfort, getting answers quickly matters. Explanation

An aortic aneurysm is a bulge or weakening in the wall of the aorta, the body's main artery. If it grows too large, it can rupture—a life-threatening emergency. Most aneurysms develop slowly without symptoms, making screening crucial for early detection.

Who Should Be Screened?

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

Current guidelines focus on AAA because it's most common and asymptomatic until advanced.

Men aged 65–75 who have ever smokedOne-time abdominal ultrasound is recommended. Even a brief smoking history increases risk.

Have smoked

Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm (TAA)

Screening recommendations for TAA are less definitive. You may need imaging if you have:

A known genetic condition (e.g., Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome)

A family history of thoracic aneurysm or dissection

Bicuspid aortic valve

When to Seek Help

Aortic aneurysms often have no symptoms. However, if you experience any of the following, seek immediate medical attention:

Sudden, severe back or abdominal pain

A pulsating sensation near the navel

Shortness of breath, chest pain (for thoracic aneurysms)

Sudden onset of dizziness or fainting

Speak to a Doctor

Screening guidelines provide a framework, but each person's health is unique. Always:

Discuss screening options with your primary care provider

Review imaging results together

Talk about personalized risk factors

Address any new or worsening symptoms promptly

Early detection and ongoing monitoring can make a life-saving difference. If you have concerns about an aortic aneurysm or any serious health issue, speak to a doctor right away.
