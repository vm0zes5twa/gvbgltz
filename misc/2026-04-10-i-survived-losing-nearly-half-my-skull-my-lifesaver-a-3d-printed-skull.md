Just over two years ago, Morrison developed an unusual headache. “I felt a pressure that wasn’t exploding, but it was never present before,” he says. His wife took him to a local New Jersey hospital, where imaging tests revealed that he had a brain bleed—a rare side effect of a blood-thinning drug he was taking for a heart rhythm problem.

Morrison was airlifted to Hackensack Meridian Jersey Shore University Medical Center. There, neurosurgeon Nitesh Patel, MD, removed part of Morrison’s skull to relieve pressure on his brain. He later performed another operation to repair the skull with plates and screws, but it wasn’t back together long. Imaging showed an additional problem: a growing brain tumor, unrelated to the original brain bleed, that needed to be removed through yet more surgery.

The problem, of course, is that your skull isn’t meant to be sawed open and repaired multiple times. After Morrison’s third brain surgery, his skull bones didn’t have the conditions required to fuse back together. Without blood flow to nourish it, a large piece of his skull started falling inward like the wall of a dilapidated building. Even the screws were bending and coming loose, leaving his brain unprotected against infections and increasing the likelihood that any head trauma, even from something like a moderate fall, would permanently damage his brain. “You have to replace it with something,” says Dr. Patel. “You can’t walk around with a crater on your head.” The old-school fix, a mesh implant, wouldn’t match the contour of Morrison’s skull due to its damage, which extended from the top of his head down the left side, near his ear. Dr. Patel needed something that could better mimic the shape of his patient’s head and be undetectable once it healed.

The surgeon pitched an idea: Using a medical-grade 3D printer, they could make a high-tech puzzle piece that fit into the skull. Morrison didn’t hesitate. From his work as an engineer, he already knew about 3D printing, which layers material to create precise shapes. “I was very much open to the idea, especially since I didn’t really have too many choices based on my original skull breaking down,” he says.

Dr. Patel shared CT scans showing the fine detail of Morrison’s skull with MedCAD, a company that 3D-prints custom implants to replace damaged skull and facial bones. “They make a 3D model and send me a mock-up, almost like an architectural drawing for a giant building,” says Dr. Patel. He reviewed the model’s shape and measurements with the company (that model, now covered with signatures from the nurses and doctors, sits inside Morrison’s New Jersey home). Then MedCAD printed the implant using a strong polymer material with properties similar to those of healthy bone.

For Morrison’s fourth surgery, Dr. Patel removed the original skull piece and screwed the implant in place, just as he had with the bone fragment three times before. Morrison recovered without complications. He credits his active lifestyle, which included weightlifting three times a week, as well as his wife (she “won’t stand for” being ensnared by challenges and low points, he says), for his ability to bounce back. “I don’t even know it’s there anymore,” Morrison says, as he goes about his three part-time jobs, working for a hardware store, a power cable company, and a school (retirement bored him, he says). He doesn’t have a full head of hair, but he doesn’t need one to conceal the evidence of his surgery—you can’t tell that a big chunk of his skull came from a 3D printer.

These implants can be life-changing and lifesaving for people with brain bleeds, brain tumors, and other problems—such as head injuries or infections—that jeopardize the integrity of their skull, says Dr. Patel. He recently used a similar implant on a patient who had a tumor behind the eye that pushed the eyeball forward. After removing the tumor, the implant restored the space behind the eye.

Researchers around the world are also working on 3D-printed implants for other body parts, including damaged jaws, as well as knee, hip, and ankle replacements, heart valves, and tiny bones in the ear.
