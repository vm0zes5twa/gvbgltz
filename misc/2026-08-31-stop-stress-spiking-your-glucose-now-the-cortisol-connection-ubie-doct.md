The Cortisol Connection: How Stress Impacts Your Glucose

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

How does stress affect blood sugar? Stress triggers cortisol release, which raises blood sugar by stimulating glucose production, reducing insulin sensitivity, and mobilizing energy stores. Short-term spikes are normal, but chronic stress can cause repeated glucose elevations, weight gain, and increased metabolic risk such as insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes.

Key factors to manage include sleep quality, physical activity, diet, and mental health support. Monitoring your glucose levels, adopting lifestyle changes, and recognizing when symptoms warrant medical attention are essential next steps.

Explanation

Stress is a normal part of life. When you're stuck in traffic, dealing with a tough deadline or managing family obligations, your body reacts by releasing hormones—chief among them cortisol. While cortisol helps you handle short-term challenges, chronically elevated levels can disrupt how your body uses glucose. This raises the question: Can stress raise blood sugar? The answer is yes, and understanding the connection can help you take control of both your stress and your blood sugar levels.

How Stress Triggers Cortisol Release

When you perceive a threat—real or imagined—your brain's hypothalamus signals the adrenal glands to pump out cortisol. This response, known as the "fight-or-flight" reaction, is designed to give you the energy to respond quickly:

Hypothalamus activates

Adrenal glands release cortisol

Cortisol increases your heart rate, sharpens focus, and mobilizes energy

In the short term, this is healthy. Cortisol ensures your muscles have fuel by raising blood glucose (sugar) and suppressing non-essential processes like digestion or immune responses.

Cortisol's Role in Glucose Metabolism

Cortisol helps maintain your blood sugar during stress by:

Stimulating gluconeogenesis: the creation of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources (e.g., amino acids) in the liver

Reducing insulin sensitivity: making it harder for cells to take in glucose, so more stays circulating in your bloodstream

Mobilizing fat and protein stores: providing additional raw materials for energy

Together, these actions ensure you have quick access to fuel when you need it most. But they also mean that each time you experience stress, your blood sugar can rise.

Can Stress Raise Blood Sugar? The Evidence

Numerous studies confirm the link between stress-induced cortisol and elevated glucose:

A 2012 study in Psychoneuroendocrinology found that even mild psychological stress can raise cortisol and blood sugar in healthy adults.

Research published by the American Diabetes Association shows that people with diabetes often experience significant glucose spikes during stressful events.

Data from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK) highlight that chronic stress is associated with poorer blood sugar control in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

This connection is especially important if you already have insulin resistance or diabetes, but it also affects healthy individuals by contributing to weight gain, fatigue and over time, increased risk for metabolic conditions.

Acute vs. Chronic Stress

Not all stress is created equal:

Acute Stress• Short-term (minutes to hours)• Brief cortisol spike, then returns to baseline• Minimal long-term impact on blood sugar if you're healthy

Chronic Stress• Persistent (days to years)• Sustained high cortisol levels• Repeated glucose spikes, increased insulin resistance

When to Speak to a Doctor

While stress-management techniques are helpful, some situations require professional medical advice:

Blood sugar readings consistently above target range

Symptoms of very high blood sugar (e.g., extreme thirst, frequent urination, nausea)

Signs of very low blood sugar (e.g., dizziness, sweating, confusion)

If you have both high stress and a chronic condition like diabetes, heart disease or hypertension

Always speak to a doctor about any serious or life-threatening concerns. They can tailor a treatment plan, adjust medications or refer you to specialists as needed.

Takeaway

Yes, stress can raise blood sugar by driving up cortisol levels and reducing insulin sensitivity. Short-term stress responses are normal, but chronic stress can lead to repeated glucose spikes, weight gain and increased risk of metabolic diseases. By combining mindful practices, regular exercise, balanced nutrition, quality sleep and professional support, you can manage both your stress and your blood sugar more effectively.
