# This Is What Having Schizophrenia Is Really Like

Delusions, hallucinations, and confusion are hallmark symptoms of schizophrenia. But even today, at a time when the general topic of mental health is more mainstream than ever, most people have trouble understanding it.

[Having Schizophrenia](https://jointrevive.xianfengchem.com/blog/5952130011.html)

Schizophrenia is a chronic brain disorder that affects fewer than 1 percent of U.S. men and women; men tend to develop it a few years earlier than women, usually in their late teens and early 20s. Symptoms vary by person, but confusion and distortions in perceptions, emotions, and behavior are common. People living with schizophrenia may see and hear things that don’t exist, which can be scary to those around them, but they are no more a danger to themselves or anyone else than someone who doesn’t have schizophrenia.

Kody Green, a motivational speaker and host of the podcast Unseen & Unheard, understood this from an early age, as he watched his mother struggle with hallucinations and other symptoms throughout his childhood. “I remember having my friends over, and my mom was talking to someone who wasn’t there,” Green says. “She said it was my dad, but he had passed away years earlier.” Seeing his friends and, later, his friends’ parents begin to form opinions about his mother was hard. “In a small town, it doesn’t take long for word to spread,” he recalls.

It wasn’t until he was in high school that his mom was diagnosed with schizoaffective disorder and later, schizophrenia, and began receiving treatment. Soon after, Green was starting to experience symptoms himself, even if he didn’t recognize them at first.

Men’s Health spoke to Green about his journey with schizophrenia—both as a caregiver and as a patient—and how he is living a full, productive life while successfully managing his symptoms.

MH: What was it like growing up with a mom who had schizoaffective disorder, especially when you were younger and you didn’t know what it was?

Green: I think the hardest part was that we lived in rural Wisconsin, where there wasn’t a lot of information or resources. Also, she was a single parent, which means my brother and I became caregivers at a very young age. But the most difficult part was that she went undiagnosed and misdiagnosed for so long. At times it felt like things were never going to get better, because we couldn’t get her a diagnosis or on the right medication, and that was really frustrating—especially when you’re just trying to get through high school. What surprised me most was that when people heard about her diagnosis they were afraid, and it never made sense to me, because all I had ever seen was a confused and scared person. She was never a danger to anyone.

MH: When did you begin to notice symptoms in yourself?

Green: Once my mom was treated and she became more stable, I decided to go off to college. I was 18 and within that first year away, I started having auditory and visual hallucinations, and that very quickly led to paranoia and delusions. I can’t pinpoint the moment exactly because of the delusional nature of the illness, but I had to drop out of school, and that’s around the same time I also fell into addiction as a way to self-medicate. I was looking for any way to escape.

MH: Given that you knew so much about schizophrenia because of your life experience, why didn’t you seek help?

Green: I still struggled to admit there was something wrong, because of the stigma surrounding schizophrenia and medication. Even when I had moments of clarity and I recalled my mom’s situation, I refused to reach out for help for those reasons. Eventually, I knew it was time and I came to terms with my diagnosis.

MH: People with schizophrenia have much higher rates of substance-use disorders. Do you think your addiction clouded your judgment?

Green: I didn’t even identify my addiction as an issue because of my mental state. At the time, I was very delusional. Looking back, I can see there were issues with the way I was going about “treating” myself. But that was the only thing I felt could help at the moment.

MH: It’s interesting that even after you saw what happened with your mom, you still weren’t quick to say, “Well, it took my mom a while to get the right diagnosis and the right treatment, but now she’s doing much better. Why don’t I just skip to that stage?”

Green: With psychosis, schizophrenia, and other neurologic conditions, there is sometimes a symptom called anosognosia that prevents a person from knowing they’re struggling with a disorder. And when you have a delusional illness like schizophrenia, things that should make sense and things that you know suddenly become very difficult to understand.

I had this wealth of knowledge from my mom’s experience, but I didn’t consider any of it in my journey. When people around me would even suggest that I had schizophrenia like my mom, I would get upset and defensive, because I didn’t think anything was wrong with me.

MH: How were you finally diagnosed?

Green: My addiction led to bad behavior, and I was eventually incarcerated. I still didn’t realize that I had schizophrenia, but I started to recognize that there were things everyone else was doing that I couldn’t do, like getting out of bed in the morning and communicating effectively with people. I started questioning whether something was wrong with me.

Finally, I tried reaching out for help, but due to the nature of our current prison systems, I wasn’t able to get any at that time. When I was released, I reached out to my mom and girlfriend, who’s now my wife, and I let them know that I thought they were right, something was wrong, and I wanted to see a doctor. Little did I know they had been stockpiling resources, so they knew what to do.

For me, the process of getting diagnosed and treated was quick because I had a genetic predisposition. If you compare my journey with my mom’s journey, it’s easy to see why early intervention is so important. It took way longer for her to receive a diagnosis, and it had a lot of negative effects on her. I started having symptoms at 18 and was diagnosed with schizophrenia at 21—that’s a fraction of the time that it took my mom to receive her diagnosis and treatment.

MH: How did your life change before and after that moment?

Green: Before my diagnosis, my entire life was my symptoms. Whether that was being paranoid that people were trying to sedate me and put me in a psychiatric facility, or whether it was delusions or hallucinations, if I was awake, I was experiencing some sort of schizophrenia symptom. Now that I’m receiving treatment, I’ll still have symptoms occasionally, but it’s once every other week instead of every waking moment. I tried different medications because treatment isn’t one size fits all for schizophrenia and it was important that I found a medication that worked for my life. I eventually found that long-acting injectable medications (LAIs) worked for me because they managed my symptoms and delayed the chance of a relapse without the daily task of taking medicine. LAIs can be given outside the hospital or doctor’s office, like your local health and wellness convenience store. Treatment returned my clarity and my ability to articulate thoughts and feelings. I didn’t think I was going to be able to work, drive, or travel, and I do all those things now.

MH: What do you think is the biggest misconception about people with schizophrenia and the disorder itself?

Green: The first one is that people assume schizophrenia makes a person inherently more dangerous than someone else. But based on my own experience, as well as my experience with my mom, we’re just very confused and afraid when we’re experiencing symptoms.

The other big misconception is that a schizophrenia diagnosis means a person can’t live a productive life, but with the right medication and treatment, they can.

As an advocate for people struggling with mental-health disorders, I receive a lot of comments about my videos and posts about schizophrenia (@schizophrenichippie) from people who assume that, because I’m doing well and living a functional life, I couldn’t really have schizophrenia. I think that shows how little people know about it, especially if you only know what you’ve seen on TV or in movies. There may be millions of people like me who are living full lives with the help of treatment and medication.
