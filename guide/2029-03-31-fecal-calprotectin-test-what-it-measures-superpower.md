What fecal calprotectin test means

Calprotectin is a calcium-binding protein that floods your intestinal tract when neutrophils (a type of white blood cell) detect threats. Think of neutrophils as your gut's first responders. When they sense bacteria, damaged tissue, or other inflammatory triggers, they release calprotectin along with other defensive compounds.

This protein survives the digestive journey intact when samples are handled promptly, making it a useful window into what's happening along your intestinal tract. Because calprotectin degrades at room temperature over several days, laboratories recommend refrigerating samples until delivery to preserve accuracy.

The fecal calprotectin test measures calprotectin concentration in micrograms per gram of stool (mcg/g). Higher concentrations indicate more neutrophil activity, which typically means more inflammation. Unlike systemic inflammation markers in blood, calprotectin reflects localized gut inflammation specifically.

This test became valuable because it bridges a diagnostic gap. Blood tests can miss intestinal inflammation, especially when it's mild or localized. Invasive procedures like colonoscopy provide direct visualization but aren't practical for routine monitoring. Calprotectin offers a middle ground: specific, non-invasive, and repeatable.

The test is particularly useful for distinguishing inflammatory conditions like inflammatory bowel disease from functional disorders like irritable bowel syndrome. This distinction matters because treatment approaches differ significantly between these categories.

How to interpret fecal calprotectin test

Most laboratories use 50 mcg/g as the upper limit of normal, though some use 60 mcg/g. Results below this threshold suggest minimal intestinal inflammation. Results above this level indicate elevated neutrophil activity in your gut.

The interpretation framework follows three general zones. Levels below 50 mcg/g typically indicate low inflammation risk. Levels between 50-200 mcg/g suggest mild to moderate inflammation that warrants investigation. Levels above 200 mcg/g often indicate significant inflammatory activity requiring medical evaluation.

However, numbers alone don't tell the complete story. A result of 75 mcg/g in someone with severe abdominal pain carries different implications than the same level in someone feeling perfectly well. Symptoms, medical history, and other test results create the complete picture.

Trending matters more than single measurements. Calprotectin levels can fluctuate based on diet, stress, medications, and minor infections. A pattern of consistently elevated results carries more weight than one isolated high reading.

Consider the clinical context carefully. Someone with known inflammatory bowel disease might use calprotectin to monitor treatment response, where declining levels indicate improvement. Someone with unexplained digestive symptoms might use it to determine whether inflammation is contributing to their issues.

What can influence fecal calprotectin test

Medications significantly impact calprotectin levels. NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) like ibuprofen can raise your levels and reduce the test's specificity. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) for acid reflux may also elevate results by altering gut bacteria and intestinal permeability.

Recent infections can leave lingering inflammatory signatures. Gastroenteritis from food poisoning or viral infections can keep calprotectin elevated after symptoms resolve, and minor bacterial overgrowth episodes can temporarily raise levels.

Related context that changes the picture

Lactoferrin is another neutrophil protein measured in stool that often correlates with calprotectin. When both markers are elevated together, it strengthens the evidence for active intestinal inflammation. Discordant results might suggest different inflammatory processes or sample collection issues.

Blood inflammatory markers provide additional context. Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) alongside high calprotectin suggests systemic inflammation extending beyond the gut. Normal blood markers with elevated calprotectin point to localized intestinal inflammation.

Microbiome testing can reveal connections. Bacterial imbalances in the gut may influence calprotectin levels, and shifts in gut flora have been linked to intestinal inflammation in various settings.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does it mean if your fecal calprotectin is high?

High fecal calprotectin (above 50-60 mcg/g) indicates active inflammation in your intestines. This occurs when neutrophils, your gut's immune cells, detect threats and release calprotectin protein. Elevated levels suggest inflammatory conditions but don't specify the exact cause, requiring further evaluation with your care team.

When should you worry about calprotectin levels?

Consider medical evaluation when calprotectin exceeds 200 mcg/g, especially with persistent digestive symptoms. Consistently elevated levels over time are more concerning than single high readings. Combine results with symptoms like persistent abdominal pain, changes in bowel habits, or unexplained weight loss to determine urgency.

What cancers can cause high calprotectin?

Colorectal cancer can elevate calprotectin levels due to tumor-related inflammation and tissue damage. However, many non-cancerous conditions also raise calprotectin, including inflammatory bowel disease, infections, and medication effects. High calprotectin alone doesn't indicate cancer but warrants further investigation when combined with other concerning symptoms.

What is a normal calprotectin level?

Normal fecal calprotectin levels are typically below 50 mcg/g, though some laboratories use 60 mcg/g as the upper limit. These levels indicate minimal intestinal inflammation. However, 'normal' can vary slightly between individuals and laboratories, so discuss your specific results with your care team for proper interpretation.

How does fecal calprotectin differ from blood-based inflammation tests?

Unlike blood tests that measure systemic inflammation and can miss mild or localized gut inflammation, fecal calprotectin reflects what is happening directly at the intestinal tissue level. This makes it a more sensitive indicator of local gut inflammation, capturing the immune response in the intestinal tract where problems actually develop rather than relying on secondary signals in the bloodstream.

What is the difference between fecal calprotectin and lactoferrin?

Lactoferrin is another neutrophil-derived protein measured in stool that often correlates with calprotectin. When both markers are elevated together, it strengthens the evidence for active intestinal inflammation. Discordant results may suggest different inflammatory processes or sample collection issues. Some laboratories offer both markers to provide confirmatory evidence of neutrophil activity in the intestinal tract.

How should I store and handle a fecal calprotectin sample?

Calprotectin degrades at room temperature over several days, so samples should be refrigerated until delivery to preserve accuracy. Most test kits specify a delivery window of a few days, though some include preservatives that extend this period. Follow the instructions provided with your collection kit and contact your laboratory if you are unsure about handling requirements.

Can stress or exercise affect calprotectin results?

Yes, chronic stress increases systemic inflammation and can elevate baseline calprotectin levels. Intense exercise temporarily increases intestinal permeability, potentially raising results in the short term, though regular moderate activity typically reduces inflammatory markers over time. Poor sleep also influences intestinal barrier function and immune activity, which can affect calprotectin readings.
